   161→V O L U M E 3
   162→Data Architecture
   163→ADR-041 to ADR-060 - 20 decisions - 34 sections each
   164→Volume 3 defines the data architecture of the PreOne platform. The 20
   165→ADRs in this volume translate the structural foundation (Volume 1) and
   166→the domain model (Volume 2) into a concrete data-persistence strategy.
   167→ADR-041 establishes PostgreSQL 16 as the sole relational store,
   168→concentrating operational expertise and simplifying DR. ADR-042
   169→standardises on UUID v7 for monotonic, time-sortable primary keys
   170→across every table. ADR-043 through ADR-046 establish the four
   171→isolation axes — tenant, school, branch, and academic year — that scope
   172→every multi-tenant query, enforced by Postgres row-level security and
   173→composite keys. ADR-047 through ADR-049 mandate soft delete, audit
   174→columns, and optimistic concurrency for every mutable entity, ensuring
   175→data integrity and recoverability. ADR-050 and ADR-051 define the
   176→indexing and partitioning strategies that keep query latency bounded as
   177→data volume grows: composite and partial indexes for hot paths, range
   178→partitioning by academic year for high-volume tables. ADR-052 governs
   179→JSONB usage for flexible schemas, balancing structure with adaptability.
   180→ADR-053 and ADR-054 define retention and archival policies that
   181→balance regulatory compliance with cost. ADR-055 and ADR-056 define
   182→the backup and restore topologies that meet the platform's 5-minute RPO
   183→and 1-hour RTO targets. ADR-057 and ADR-058 govern schema
   184→migration discipline (Flyway, versioned, forward-only) and seed data
   185→strategy. ADR-059 and ADR-060 close the volume with the search
   186→strategy (Postgres FTS first, Elasticsearch as optional accelerator) and
   187→the reporting database strategy (read-optimised replica fed by logical
   188→replication, isolating analytical load from OLTP). Together, these 20
   189→ADRs are the data-architecture playbook of the PreOne platform — the
   190→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 5

   199→DECISION SUMMARY
   200→DECISION
   201→Adopt PostgreSQL 16 as the sole relational data store for the PreOne
   202→platform across OLTP, audit, configuration, and reporting-adjacent
   203→workloads. All persistence layers, including the modular monolith's
   204→bounded contexts, will use Postgres via a shared cluster topology with read
   205→replicas, connection pooling through PgBouncer, and the PostGIS,
   206→pgvector, pg_partman, and pg_stat_statements extensions enabled by
   207→default. MongoDB, MySQL, and DynamoDB are rejected as primary stores.

   218→CONTEXT
   219→PreOne is a multi-tenant SaaS platform serving the K-12 and higher-education
   220→sectors across multiple geographies. The data layer must support transactional
   221→workloads (enrollment, grading, attendance), bulk reporting (academic
   222→transcripts, attendance rosters), audit trails (every mutation to a regulated
   223→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 6
   224→
   225→PreOne ADR - Volume 3: Data Architecture v3.0
   226→entity), full-text and semantic search (institution directory, course catalog), and
   227→time-series-ish telemetry (daily attendance aggregates). The previous platform
   228→generation used a mix of MongoDB for documents and MySQL for transactions,
   229→which produced consistency, operational, and hiring problems. PostgreSQL
   230→16, released in September 2023 and production-hardened by mid-2024, brings
   231→logical replication improvements, parallel query enhancements, SQL/JSON
   232→path expressions, and improved vacuum feedback that materially affect our
   233→workload profile. The PostGIS extension covers the geolocation needs of the
   234→school-locator service; pgvector covers the semantic-search needs of the AI-
   235→assistant roadmap (ADR-148); pg_partman automates the range-partition
   236→maintenance mandated by ADR-051. The engineering organisation is already
   237→fluent in SQL and in the Postgres operational model. Standardising on a single
   238→relational engine reduces the cognitive surface area for on-call, simplifies the
   239→disaster-recovery story (one PITR topology, one backup format), and
   240→concentrates tooling investment. The principal alternative considered was a
   241→polyglot persistence strategy (Postgres for OLTP, Elasticsearch for search,
   242→DynamoDB for session state), but the operational cost of three engines for a 50-
   243→engineer organisation was judged prohibitive.
   244→BUSINESS DRIVERS
   245→PreOne's institutional customers sign annual contracts with strict uptime and
   246→data-recoverability SLAs; data-layer incidents are the single largest contributor
   247→to churn. Consolidating on Postgres concentrates operational expertise,
   248→shortens on-call escalation paths, and reduces the number of backup and DR
   249→topologies the SRE team must rehearse. The savings are redirected into higher-
   250→value platform work. A second driver is the international expansion planned for
   251→2026, which requires data residency in India, the EU, and the US. Postgres runs
   252→identically in AWS, Azure, and on-premises; a polyglot strategy would multiply
   253→the residency certification work. A single engine also simplifies the auditors'
   254→job: one schema-introspection script, one retention enforcement job, one
   255→access-control model. A tertiary driver is the AI roadmap. Embeddings, vector
   256→similarity search, and structured retrieval all benefit from co-location with the
   257→transactional data, avoiding the dual-write consistency problems that arise
   258→when vectors live in a separate store. pgvector inside Postgres gives us ACID
   259→guarantees over embedding updates without a separate vector database.
   260→PROBLEM STATEMENT
   261→PreOne requires a single, well-understood relational data platform that can
   262→serve transactional, audit, search, and reporting workloads under a multi-
   263→tenant model while keeping operational complexity, hiring cost, and DR
   264→rehearsal burden within the capacity of a 50-engineer organisation.

   268→CONSTRAINTS
   269→● All regulated data must reside in PostgreSQL; no shadow databases
   270→holding PII or academic records.
   271→● The engine must support logical replication for tenant-scoped
   272→extraction and cross-region replication for DR.
   273→● The engine must support range and list partitioning for high-volume
   274→tables (attendance, gradebook, audit log).
   275→● Extensions required: PostGIS, pgvector, pg_partman,
   276→pg_stat_statements, pgcrypto, uuid-ossp or pg_idkit.
   277→● Connection pooling is mandatory; no direct application connection to
   278→the primary without PgBouncer in transaction mode.
   279→● Major version upgrades occur on a 24-month cadence with one minor
   280→version lag from upstream GA.
   281→ASSUMPTIONS
   282→● PostgreSQL 16 will receive security support through November 2028,
   283→sufficient for our upgrade cadence.
   284→● AWS RDS for PostgreSQL and Aurora PostgreSQL will continue to
   285→support the extensions we depend on.
   286→● The engineering organisation's Postgres fluency will scale linearly with
   287→headcount; no sudden skill cliff.
   288→● pgvector performance will remain competitive with dedicated vector
   289→databases for our workload size (≤ 100M vectors per tenant).
   290→● Logical replication will remain stable enough for tenant-extraction use
   291→cases without bespoke conflict resolution.

   353→ADOPTED
   354→Adopt PostgreSQL 16 as the sole relational data store for the PreOne
   355→platform across transactional, audit, configuration, and reporting-adjacent
   356→workloads. We will run it on AWS RDS for PostgreSQL for the primary
   357→cluster, with cross-region read replicas for DR and Aurora PostgreSQL as a
   358→documented future migration target. PgBouncer in transaction-pooling
   359→mode will front every application connection. The PostGIS, pgvector,
   360→pg_partman, and pg_stat_statements extensions are enabled by default on
   361→every database. Major version upgrades occur on a 24-month cadence. No
   362→new workloads will be written against MongoDB or MySQL; existing
   363→workloads will be migrated per the plan in ADR-057.
   364→DETAILED RATIONALE
   365→PostgreSQL 16 was chosen over the alternatives because it is the only engine
   366→that satisfies all five of PreOne's hard requirements simultaneously: ACID
   367→transactions with serializable isolation, native UUID generation, robust range
   368→and list partitioning, JSONB for flexible schemas, and a first-party vector
   369→extension (pgvector) that co-locates embeddings with transactional data. The
   370→polyglot alternative was attractive on theoretical grounds (best engine per
   371→workload) but fails on operational grounds. PreOne's SRE team has four
   372→engineers; running three engines well requires six. The polyglot path also
   373→introduces dual-write consistency problems between Postgres and
   374→Elasticsearch that are well documented in the industry and that we have
   375→personally experienced in the legacy platform: out-of-sync search indexes,
   376→repair jobs that run for hours, and customer-visible staleness during incident
   377→recovery. Co-locating search in Postgres via FTS (ADR-059) and using
   378→Elasticsearch only as an optional accelerator where Postgres FTS cannot meet
   379→latency targets eliminates the dual-write problem. MySQL 8.4 was a serious
   380→contender. The team has prior MySQL experience and AWS pricing favours
   381→MySQL slightly. However, MySQL's JSON type lacks the GIN-indexed JSONB
   382→operations that PreOne's flexible-schema use cases depend on (ADR-052),
   383→MySQL's partitioning cannot partition by an expression in the way PreOne's
   384→academic-year partitioning requires (ADR-051), and MySQL has no equivalent
   385→to pgvector for the AI roadmap (ADR-148). Each gap alone is bridgeable;
   386→together they represent a three-year strategic disadvantage. The 'defer' option
   387→was rejected because every quarter of deferral adds to the operational debt.
   388→The legacy MongoDB cluster requires dedicated on-call, dedicated backup
   389→rehearsal, and dedicated patching. Each of those activities consumes engineer-
   390→weeks per quarter that would otherwise fund platform investment. The Aurora
   391→PostgreSQL variant was considered as the primary rather than RDS. Aurora's
   392→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 10
   393→
   394→PreOne ADR - Volume 3: Data Architecture v3.0
   395→storage architecture (distributed storage, fast replica promotion) is attractive,
   396→but Aurora's extension support lags RDS by 3-6 months and Aurora's cost model
   397→is unfavourable at the relatively modest data volumes PreOne currently holds.
   398→We will revisit Aurora when total dataset size exceeds 5 TB or when cross-
   399→region read replica lag becomes a constraint; the trigger is documented in the
   400→futureEvolution section.

   452→DATABASE IMPACT
   453→Every bounded context in the modular monolith will own exactly one schema in
   454→the primary Postgres cluster. Cross-schema foreign keys are forbidden; cross-
   455→schema references use UUID columns without FK constraints and are validated
   456→at the application layer (ADR-043). Tables must use UUID v7 primary keys
   457→(ADR-042), include the standard audit columns (ADR-048), include a deleted_at
   458→column for soft delete (ADR-047), and include a version column for optimistic
   459→concurrency (ADR-049). High-volume tables (attendance, gradebook,
   460→audit_log) must be range-partitioned by academic_year (ADR-051). The
   461→pgvector and PostGIS extensions create their own catalog objects; tenant
   462→migrations must include extension availability checks. The shared cluster
   463→topology means noisy-neighbour risk between contexts is real; context owners
   464→must use statement_timeout and cost-based admission control to protect
   465→shared capacity.
   466→API IMPACT
   467→API contracts do not change as a direct consequence of this ADR, but the
   468→persistence choice shapes several API conventions. Pagination must use keyset
   469→pagination over UUID v7 (which is monotonically increasing) rather than
   470→OFFSET, because OFFSET on large tables is unbounded in cost. Bulk endpoints
   471→must use COPY through the Postgres binary protocol rather than INSERT
   472→loops; the API gateway exposes a /bulk endpoint family for this purpose. Error
   473→responses that surface database constraint violations must map Postgres
   474→SQLSTATE codes to HTTP status codes deterministically (23505 -> 409, 23503
   475→-> 422, 40001 -> 409 with retry-after). Read-after-write consistency for APIs
   476→that read from replicas is governed by ADR-060 (Reporting Database Strategy)
   477→and requires clients to send an X-Read-Consistency header when they need
   478→primary reads.
   479→UI IMPACT
   480→The PostgreSQL strategy has no direct UI surface — it is infrastructure.
   481→Indirectly, UI consistency benefits because all reads return from a single
   482→engine with consistent transaction semantics; users never see partial writes or
   483→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 12
   484→
   485→PreOne ADR - Volume 3: Data Architecture v3.0
   486→stale reads across engines. Reporting dashboards (which query read replicas)
   487→may lag the primary by up to 5 seconds; UI components that surface time-
   488→sensitive data (e.g., a parent app showing 'just-marked' attendance) display a
   489→'syncing' indicator when the lag exceeds 2 seconds, set via the lag-threshold
   490→endpoint. No UI changes were required for the migration itself; the application
   491→API is engine-agnostic.
   492→SECURITY IMPACT
   493→Consolidating on Postgres concentrates the security surface in one engine,
   494→which is a net positive: one set of role definitions, one row-level-security policy
   495→framework, one audit pipeline. Row-level security (RLS) is mandatory for any
   496→table that contains PII and is shared across tenants; tenant isolation RLS
   497→policies are defined in ADR-043. Connection strings are issued per service per
   498→environment and stored in AWS Secrets Manager with 30-day rotation. The
   499→postgres superuser role is never granted to applications; the highest privilege
   500→any application holds is the owner of its own schema. The pg_stat_statements
   501→extension exposes query text, which can leak PII in logs; this is mitigated by
   502→redacting parameters before they reach pg_stat_statements via the
   503→log_statement_sample_rate knob and by scrubbing pg_stat_statements dumps
   504→before they leave the cluster.
   505→PERFORMANCE IMPACT
   506→PostgreSQL 16 on RDS with the configuration specified in the operational
   507→runbook sustains approximately 18,000 TPS on the primary for our
   508→transactional workload (enrollment, attendance, grading) at p99 latency of
   509→12ms for indexed point lookups and 85ms for the median reporting query.
   510→Connection pooling through PgBouncer in transaction mode allows 400
   511→application connections to share 50 backend connections, reducing memory
   512→pressure on the primary. Parallel query (max_parallel_workers_per_gather=4)
   513→reduces the median aggregate reporting query by 35% versus serial execution.
   514→The pgvector HNSW index delivers p99 vector similarity latency of 18ms for
   515→1M-vector tenant scope. The principal performance risk is autovacuum falling
   516→behind on the high-churn audit_log and attendance tables; this is mitigated by
   517→per-table autovacuum scaling factors documented in ADR-050.
   518→SCALABILITY ANALYSIS
   519→Vertical scaling on RDS reaches its ceiling at db.r6g.16xlarge (64 vCPU, 512
   520→GB RAM); we are currently at db.r6g.8xlarge, leaving one doubling of vertical
   521→headroom. Beyond that, horizontal scaling proceeds through read replicas (we
   522→currently run two, can scale to fifteen) and through logical partitioning at the
   523→application layer (tenant-sharding across multiple Postgres clusters). The
   524→trigger for tenant-sharding is documented in ADR-043: when total dataset size
   525→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 13
   526→
   527→PreOne ADR  -  Volume 3: Data Architecture  v3.0
   528→exceeds 5 TB or when the primary sustains > 60% CPU at peak, we shard by
   529→tenant cohort. The pgvector workload scales through HNSW index partitioning
   530→per tenant; the 100M-vectors-per-tenant ceiling is the documented soft limit
   531→beyond which we will move that workload to a dedicated vector store. Logical
   532→replication lag to read replicas is monitored; sustained lag > 5 seconds triggers
   533→investigation.

   630→MIGRATION PLAN
   631→Migration from the legacy MongoDB + MySQL mix to Postgres proceeds in four
   632→phases over four quarters. Phase 1 (Q1): stand up the Postgres 16 primary, read
   633→replicas, PgBouncer, and the S3-based backup topology; migrate the three
   634→lowest-risk bounded contexts (configuration, notifications, feature-flags) as the
   635→pilot. Phase 2 (Q2): migrate the audit-log and reference-data contexts, which
   636→are high-volume but low-complexity; this exercises the partitioning,
   637→autovacuum, and backup-verification machinery at scale. Phase 3 (Q3): migrate
   638→the enrollment and gradebook contexts, which are the highest-complexity
   639→transactional workloads; dual-write during cut-over with reconciliation jobs.
   640→Phase 4 (Q4): decommission MongoDB and MySQL; archive the final snapshots
   641→to S3 Glacier with a 7-year retention per ADR-053. Rollback per phase is to the
   642→prior engine for 30 days after cut-over, after which the legacy snapshots
   643→become read-only.
   644→TESTING STRATEGY
   645→Each migrated context is tested at three layers before cut-over. (1) Schema
   646→equivalence: a property-based test compares the set of queryable predicates
   647→between the legacy and Postgres schemas; any gap is a blocker. (2) Query
   648→equivalence: 5% of production read traffic is shadowed to the Postgres replica
   649→and the result sets are compared row-by-row; mismatch rate must be below
   650→0.01%. (3) Transactional equivalence: a synthetic workload replays 24 hours of
   651→production write traffic against a Postgres mirror and verifies post-conditions.
   652→After cut-over, the legacy engine is kept in read-only shadow mode for 30 days
   653→with nightly reconciliation. The Postgres platform itself is tested via the
   654→standard RDS build pipeline plus the chaos rehearsal documented in ADR-150
   655→(Performance Testing).
   656→MONITORING & OBSERVABILITY

   751→UUID Strategy
   752→Volume 3 — Data Architecture - Data Architecture

   754→DECISION SUMMARY
   755→DECISION
   756→Adopt UUID v7 as the standard primary key type across every table in the
   757→PreOne Postgres cluster. UUID v7 combines a 48-bit Unix millisecond
   758→timestamp prefix with 74 bits of randomness, producing identifiers that are
   759→globally unique, time-ordered, and indexable without the hot-spot pathology
   760→of UUID v4. bigint identity columns remain only for legacy tables pending
   761→migration; new tables must use UUID v7. The pg_idkit extension provides
   762→the generator function.

   773→CONTEXT
   774→Primary key choice has consequences that propagate across the data layer:
   775→index locality, replication topology, key-shardability, client-side generation,
   776→and URL stability. PreOne's legacy platform used a mix of MySQL auto-
   777→increment bigint, MongoDB ObjectId, and UUID v4, with predictably messy
   778→results: foreign-key columns with mismatched types, indexes with poor locality,
   779→PreOne ADR Series - Phase 4 / 10 - Vol 3: Data Architecture - 19
   780→
   781→PreOne ADR - Volume 3: Data Architecture v3.0
   782→and a class of bugs where client-generated ObjectIds collided with server-
   783→generated sequences. PostgreSQL 16 does not yet ship a built-in UUID v7
   784→generator (the uuid_v7() function arrives in PostgreSQL 18), so we require an
   785→extension. The pg_idkit extension is the de facto community standard, is
   786→maintained, and exposes a uuid_generate_v7() function whose output is the
   787→standard 128-bit UUID with the version nibble correctly set. UUID v7's
   788→structure means the first 48 bits are millisecond timestamp, the next 12 bits are
   789→random, and the remaining 62 bits are random; the result is monotonically
   790→increasing within a millisecond window. The principal alternative was bigint
   791→identity (auto-increment). Bigint is smaller (8 bytes vs 16), produces perfectly
   792→ordered indexes, and is well understood. Its weaknesses for our use case are: it
   793→leaks row-count information through sequential IDs (a tenant who sees
   794→enrollment_id 47 can infer prior volume), it requires a centralised generator
   795→that becomes a contention point under high write concurrency, and it cannot be
   796→generated client-side without a round trip. UUID v7 addresses all three: no
   797→information leakage, no centralised generator, and client-side generation is
   798→safe.
